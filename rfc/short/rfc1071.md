**Purpose:** Defines the *Internet checksum*, a 16-bit one's-complement sum used by IP, UDP, and TCP, and documents the mathematical properties that allow it to be computed efficiently and order-independently. The checksum value is the one's complement of the one's-complement sum of the 16-bit words of the protected data.

**Scope:** This short RFC is the reference for the checksum algorithm itself, not for any single protocol's framing. The same algorithm is reused verbatim by OSPFv2 (RFC 2328) for the OSPF packet header Checksum field (see Scope: OSPF below). It is also the IP header checksum, the UDP checksum, and the TCP checksum.

## Algorithm (RFC 1071 Section 1)

The checksum is the 16-bit one's complement of the one's-complement sum of all 16-bit words in the protected region. To **generate** the checksum:

1. Set the checksum field of the data to zero.
2. Pair adjacent octets to form 16-bit integers (the first/even octet is the high byte of each 16-bit word). If the data has an odd number of octets, pad with a single zero octet on the right *for the purpose of the sum only* (the pad is not transmitted).
3. Form the one's-complement sum of these 16-bit integers: accumulate them and, whenever the sum overflows 16 bits, add the carry-out back into the low-order bit (this is the *end-around carry* of one's-complement arithmetic). Equivalently, accumulate in a wider register and fold the carries in at the end (see Folding below).
4. Take the one's complement (bitwise NOT) of the resulting 16-bit sum.
5. Store that value in the checksum field.

To **verify** a received checksum (Section 1): compute the one's-complement sum over the same set of octets, *including* the now-populated checksum field. If the result is all-ones (-0 in one's-complement arithmetic, i.e. 0xFFFF), the check succeeds; the receiver does not need to invert and compare.

## Folding a Wide Accumulator (RFC 1071 Section 1, Section 2)

On a two's-complement machine the simplest implementation accumulates 16-bit words into a 32-bit (or 64-bit) register without carry-sensing on each add, then folds:

1. Add the high 16 bits of the accumulator to the low 16 bits.
2. Repeat step 1 until the high 16 bits are zero (one repeat suffices for a 32-bit accumulator holding a sum of 16-bit words, but a loop is the safe general form).
3. The low 16 bits now hold the one's-complement sum; invert them to get the checksum.

This "deferred carry" approach (Section 2) avoids a carry-sensing instruction in the inner loop and is the basis of the parallel-summation technique below.

## Key Properties (RFC 1071 Section 2)

| Property | Statement (Section 2) | Consequence |
|----------|-----------------------|-------------|
| Commutative + associative | The one's-complement sum may be done in any order and arbitrarily split into groups, *as long as the even/odd (high/low byte) assignment of each octet is respected*. | The protected region can be summed in pieces (e.g. header then body) and the partial sums combined; ordering of words does not change the result. |
| Byte-order (byte-swap) | The 16-bit words may be summed in either byte order; consistently swapping the bytes of every word simply rotates the bits within the final sum and does not change their internal ordering. | A little-endian or big-endian machine may sum in its native byte order and byte-swap the single final result, rather than byte-swapping every word. |
| Parallel summation | On machines with word sizes that are multiples of 16 bits, sum using the native word size, then fold the wide sum down to 16 bits by adding its 16-bit segments (each such add may produce a new end-around carry that must be folded in too). | Fewer, wider adds per loop iteration; the folding step (above) reconciles the carries at the end. |
| Incremental update | When a single header field changes, the checksum need not be recomputed from scratch; adjust it from the difference of the old and new word values. | Routers that mutate a field (e.g. TTL) update the checksum in constant time. RFC 1071 gives `C' = C + (m' - m)` in one's-complement arithmetic; RFC 1624 corrects the exact form to avoid the -0/+0 edge case. |

These four properties together are why the checksum is cheap: a long region can be split, summed out of order, summed in native byte order, and patched in place, all without altering the result.

## Scope: OSPF Packet Header Checksum (RFC 2328 Section A.3.1)

OSPFv2 reuses the RFC 1071 algorithm for the 16-bit Checksum field in the common OSPF packet header, with these protocol-specific rules:

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Coverage | The standard IP (one's-complement) checksum is computed over the *entire OSPF packet*, **excluding** the 64-bit Authentication field. |
| Checksum field during computation | The Checksum field itself is set to zero before the sum is computed (per the generate procedure above). |
| AuType 2 (cryptographic authentication) | The Checksum field is **not** used and is set to zero entirely; integrity is provided by the cryptographic authentication data instead. |
| Verification | Receivers recompute over the same coverage (excluding the Authentication field) and compare. |

The algorithm and its properties are unchanged from RFC 1071; OSPF only fixes *which* octets are covered (all but the 8-byte Authentication field) and the zero-during-computation convention.
